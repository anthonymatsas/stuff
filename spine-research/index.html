01

Daily routine

10–15 min · most days

The foundation. Do this even on good days — especially on good days. Consistency matters more than intensity.

Mobility — Morning

Prone press-ups
10 reps · 2-sec hold at top · cobra-style, elbows then hands
Stop if leg symptoms increase. Continue if symptoms centralize toward your spine.
Cat-cow
10 reps · slow tempo
Single knee to chest
5 reps each side · 5-sec hold
Half-kneeling hip flexor stretch
30 sec each side · stay tall, tuck pelvis

Core endurance — McGill big three

Modified curl-up
3 sets · 8-sec holds · descending reps 10/8/6
One knee bent, hands under low back. Lift head & shoulders only slightly.
Side plank
3 holds each side · 10–20 sec · from knees → progress to feet
Bird dog
3 sets · 8 reps each side · 8-sec hold
Keep hips square to floor — no rotation. Imagine balancing a glass on your low back.

Glutes & posterior chain

Glute bridges
2 × 12 · 2-sec hold at top · squeeze glutes
Clamshells (band) or side-lying abduction
2 × 15 each side
02

Strength block

20–30 min · 2–3× weekly

Add on top of the daily routine, or substitute for the core work some days. This builds the resilience your spine needs to handle real-life loading — including a 30-lb toddler.

Hip mobility

90/90 hip stretch
1 min each side
Pigeon or figure-4 stretch
1 min each side
Thoracic rotations
10 each side · quadruped or seated

Strength progression

Goblet squat
3 × 8–10 · light DB or KB
Hip hinge dominant, neutral spine, knees track over toes.
Romanian deadlift
3 × 8 · light load
Critical lift — this teaches the hip hinge that protects your spine when picking things up.
Suitcase carry
30–60 sec each side · weight in one hand
Mimics asymmetric toddler-carrying. Resist the lean.
Step-ups
2 × 8 each leg · controlled descent
Wall sit
2–3 holds · 30–45 sec

Cardio

Walking
20–30 min · most days
Excellent for disc health. If desk-bound, stand & move every 30–45 min.
03

Nerve glides

For L5 / S1 numbness

A "floss" rather than a stretch. Useful for radicular leg/foot symptoms.

Sciatic nerve slider — affected side
10 slow reps · 1–2× per day
Lying on back, knee bent toward chest. Slowly straighten knee while looking up; bend knee while tucking chin to chest. The two ends move together so the nerve glides without being pulled. Stop if it increases symptoms.
04

What to avoid

Risk reduction

Disc-unfriendly movements

  • Heavy spinal flexion under load — toe touches with weight, sit-ups, loaded forward bending.
  • Prolonged sitting, especially in soft chairs or slouched positions.
  • Twisting under load. Bending + twisting + load is the worst combination for disc bulges.
  • High-impact activities during flares — running, jumping, plyometrics.
  • Deep hamstring stretching while leg symptoms are active — can tension an already-irritated nerve.
05

Lifting a young child

Daily parental loading

How a parent or caregiver handles a young child every day probably matters more than the exercises. A 30-lb toddler held at arms' length loads the spine like 200+ lbs.

Picking them up off the floor

Squat down — hinge at the hips and knees, not the lower back. Chest stays up.

Bring the child close to your body before standing. Distance from your spine multiplies load dramatically.

Brace your core (think: someone's about to poke your belly) before initiating the lift.

Drive up through your legs. Never lift from a twisted position — square up first.

Carrying

Symmetric carrying (both arms, child centered) is easiest on your spine. Front carriers help.

If you hip-carry, alternate sides regularly. Most parents have a dominant hip and load it asymmetrically for years. If your symptoms are unilateral, watch which side you favor — you may be loading the painful side without realizing it.

Hip carrying forces side-bending and weight-shifting onto one leg — exactly what irritates inflamed structures.

Car seat — major culprit for parents

Loading a child into a rear-facing car seat is the perfect storm: forward bending + twisting + load.

Get as close as possible. Climb partway into the back seat if you need to. Don't reach.

Brace your core, hinge at the hips, keep the child against your chest. Move your feet to turn instead of twisting your trunk.

Floor play

Slumped sitting on the floor is a slow aggravator. Try side-sitting (legs to one side, alternate periodically), kneeling, or half-kneeling. A low stool also helps. Vary positions every few minutes.

Crib transfers

Lowering a sleeping child into a crib = forward bend with load. Drop the rail if your crib has one. If not, this is one of the worst recurring movements in your day. Brace, hinge from hips, get as close as physically possible to the mattress.

06

Flare management

When things get worse

When symptoms flare up

  • Don't go to bed rest. 1–2 days of relative rest at most, then gentle movement.
  • Walk frequently in short bouts — 5–10 min several times a day beats one long walk.
  • Pause loaded exercises (squats, deadlifts, carries) for several days. Keep doing daily mobility & gentle core work.
  • Ice for acute soreness, heat for muscle tightness. Either is fine — use what feels right.
  • Return to normal activity progressively over 5–10 days. Don't try to skip the ramp-up.
07

Use PT sessions for

Highest-value asks

What to prioritize

  1. Form check on goblet squats and Romanian deadlifts — highest stakes for getting right.
  2. Form check on the McGill big three (curl-up, side plank, bird dog).
  3. Have them watch you simulate a child pick-up and a car-seat transfer.
  4. Confirm whether prone press-ups are appropriate for your specific presentation (directional preference testing).
  5. Ask about how to progress loads and volume over the next 3–6 months.

Stop & seek emergency care if

Saddle anesthesia — numbness in the groin, inner thighs, or genitals. New bladder or bowel dysfunction — incontinence, retention, or loss of sensation. Rapidly progressing bilateral leg weakness. These can indicate cauda equina syndrome, which is rare but time-critical. ER, not "call the office."


Lower-urgency reasons to call your physician's office: fever, new significant leg weakness (foot drop, leg giving way), spreading numbness to new areas, or symptoms increasingly unresponsive to what used to help.
